I consider myself incredibly lucky to live in a world where information is so easy to obtain. I don't have to buy books, travel or contact people, to learn about a place or idea. I'm even more spoiled now that I have a cell phone with advanced wireless technology, so I can look up almost anything at any given time.

Over the past 20 years, we've shifted from a culture where most news and stories were funneled through a few outlets that researched, fact checked and verified them. These stories were high quality, but there was an actual physical limit to the amount of space stories could be given, and if organizations couldn't justify the costs or find the space, stories would be left on the cutting room floor.

But now that we have the internet, and that access has become widespread, we're treated to new stories and ideas we had almost no opportunity of finding before. They were there, but again required physical work by people to find. The New York Times only has enough money for so many pages of paper and only so many words fit on those pages. The internet has opened the floodgates to all kinds of stories from people of all walks of life. It's easier than ever before to connect with people unlike ourselves and learn about each other and the conflicts we face.

But while the amount of information has grown, we've been on a parallel track that's established personal bubbles that are increasingly hard to escape. With so much information available, services started to pop up to help us weed out the things we weren't interested in, but the side effect has secluded us from viewpoints other than our own. Whether it be Facebook, Twitter, or any other social network, we're only exposed to the information our friends share or the service thinks we'd like to see. While it's easier than ever to learn about new viewpoints, it's increasingly difficult to find them within our personal echo chambers.

It seems in trying to solve the problem of too much information, we've created a house of mirrors that primarily reflects back our own perceptions of reality.
