## The Problem

The product had grown past the point where a single onboarding email could do the job. New users needed walkthroughs, troubleshooting guides, and best-practice articles — and pieces of all three already existed, scattered across a help desk tool, a marketing blog, and a handful of internal docs nobody outside the company could see.

The real problem wasn't a lack of content. It was that three different teams had been quietly creating overlapping content for the same audience with no shared plan, which meant contradictions, duplicate effort, and no clear place to send a confused user.

## The Approach

I spent the first few weeks just mapping who owned what — which felt more like diplomacy than content strategy some days. Once I had that picture, I proposed a single content hub with clear content types (guides, troubleshooting, concepts) and clear ownership per type, so future content wouldn't immediately fragment again.

I also worked directly with support to mine their ticket history for the questions people actually asked, which shaped the initial article list far more than anyone's assumptions did. Nothing went into the first version of the hub that wasn't tied to a real, recurring question.

## The Outcome

The hub launched with about 40 articles instead of the 150 someone had originally proposed, on the theory that a smaller, accurate library beats a large messy one. Support saw a drop in repeat tickets on the topics we covered first, and — maybe more importantly — the three teams had an actual process for deciding where new content belonged going forward, instead of relitigating it every time.
